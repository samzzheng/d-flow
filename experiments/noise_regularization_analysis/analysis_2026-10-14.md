# Why DLR D-Flow resists measurement noise

The completed single-circle experiment supports **implicit regularization through restricted noise fitting**. It does not establish that DLR always supplies a cleaner gradient, changes the true generator Jacobian's rank, or achieves better accuracy than a suitably stopped exact-gradient method.

This investigation reads all 4,320 completed method histories (1,440 paired cases) and runs small CPU diagnostics. It leaves the GPU training and reconstruction queues unchanged. Protocol: six resolutions, CT10/MRI4, 30 samples per cell, 1/5/10/20% noise, five Euler steps, rank-8 fast DLR, analytical alpha=1-t, reused right basis, and shared LBFGS/Armijo settings. No new reconstruction trajectories were run.

## Evidence from trajectories

The following medians pool the same 180 cases per problem at 20% noise. Errors are relative image L2 percentages. Detailed tables retain every resolution separately.

| Problem | Method | Final error | Best along trajectory, using GT | First residual <= noise norm, else final |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| CT | D-Flow | 117.99 | 3.14 | 4.31 |
| CT | P-Flow | 10.39 | 4.60 | 5.79 |
| CT | DLR D-Flow | 5.94 | 3.77 | 5.23 |
| MRI | D-Flow | 24.02 | 1.78 | 1.96 |
| MRI | P-Flow | 25.40 | 2.11 | 2.34 |
| MRI | DLR D-Flow | 2.40 | 2.23 | 2.44 |

The best checkpoint is an oracle diagnostic, selected retrospectively using the ground truth. The discrepancy replay uses only the known experimental noise norm. Cases without a threshold crossing retain their final iterate: no difficult cases are dropped. D-Flow reaches the threshold in 166/180 CT and 157/180 MRI cases. The rule is threshold sensitive: multiplying the threshold by 1.05 instead gives D-Flow medians 16.15% CT and 7.47% MRI at 20% noise. This is a retrospective stopping analysis, not a validated universal stopping policy.

Different final attempt counts are not the whole explanation. At each case's last attempt completed by both D-Flow and DLR, their respective 20%-noise medians are 71.97% versus 5.91% for CT, and 17.39% versus 2.48% for MRI. DLR wins 153/180 and 137/180 of these paired comparisons. Accepted steps and directions can still differ.

There are two distinct types of bad reconstruction. At 20% CT, 133 of D-Flow's 147 final errors above 20% previously passed below 10%; 32 of DLR's 33 errors above 20% never passed below 10%. At 20% MRI, those counts are 104/126 for D-Flow and 0/46 for DLR. DLR therefore has substantial failures to reach a good reconstruction even though it usually avoids subsequent noise fitting. Those failures should not all be called optimizer failures: subspace bias, initialization, and inverse-problem ambiguity remain possible explanations.

## Direct evidence that measurements fit noise

Write epsilon=y-A(x_GT), and measure the coefficient of known noise in the final clean-measurement error:

\[
c=\frac{\operatorname{Re}\langle\epsilon,A(\hat x)-A(x_{GT})\rangle}{\|\epsilon\|^2}.
\]

Here c=1 means the error contains the entire component along the realized noise vector; c=0 means none of that component. It is not an image-error percentage, and other error components may remain. This calculation subtracts complex MRI arrays before taking norms.

For all 30 single-circle samples at 64 squared and 20% noise, median c is:

| Problem | D-Flow | P-Flow | DLR D-Flow |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| CT10 | 0.992 | 0.048 | 0.008 |
| MRI4 | 0.662 | 0.714 | 0.004 |

This directly supports noise fitting as the reason for many poor final D-Flow images. P-Flow behaves differently across CT and MRI; it is not uniformly noise sensitive. All 240 cases at 64 squared across four noise levels are preserved in the alignment CSV. The residual decomposition check has maximum relative error 4.2e-15.

## What low rank changes

All methods use the same generator G(z), the actual five-step Euler map. At the same source z they have the same true Jacobian J=DG(z). With g_x=A*(AG(z)-y), their gradient estimators are

\[
g_D=J^\top g_x,\qquad g_P=g_x,\qquad
g_{DLR}=\widehat J^\top g_x,\qquad \widehat J=USV^\top.
\]

The terminal analytical alpha is exactly zero, hence rank(hat J)<=8 by construction. Reporting that rank alone provides no evidence for denoising. The fast factors are not a certified truncated SVD of J. Comparing true J at different methods' final sources also mixes estimator effects with the different trajectories.

For ordinary gradient descent, the local image changes are

\[
\Delta x_D\simeq-\eta JJ^\top g_x,\qquad
\Delta x_P\simeq-\eta Jg_x,\qquad
\Delta x_{DLR}\simeq-\eta J\widehat J^\top g_x.
\]

The last expression is generally not -eta hat J hat J^T g_x. It becomes that expression for an exact singular-value truncation of J. With LBFGS, the actual local expression instead contains its inverse-Hessian action H_k:

\[
\Delta x_{DLR}\simeq-\eta JH_k\widehat J^\top g_x.
\]

The gradient lies in span(V), but H_k can mix historical directions, and V changes between evaluations. Thus the current method does not enforce a single fixed rank-8 source or image manifold.

## An exact mathematical condition for denoising

At a fixed source, define the exact clean gradient g_clean and let n=g_noisy-g_clean=-J^T A*epsilon. For the ideal projection P=VV^T, orthogonality gives

\[
\boxed{
\|P g_{noisy}-g_{clean}\|^2-\|g_{noisy}-g_{clean}\|^2
=\|(I-P)g_{clean}\|^2-\|(I-P)n\|^2.
}
\]

Projection is beneficial exactly when it discards more noise-gradient energy than clean-gradient energy. This is an algebraic statement for each fixed source and noise realization, with no Gaussian assumption.

Actual DLR also changes the coefficients within span(V). Writing c_g=g_DLR-P g_noisy, the right side gains

\[
\|c_g\|^2+2\langle Pn,c_g\rangle.
\]

Consequently, rank restriction alone is insufficient. Subspace selection, coefficient accuracy, and their interaction with noise matter.

A complementary statistical identity holds for fixed J and hat J and fresh zero-mean noise with covariance C_epsilon:

\[
\mathbb E\|\widehat J^\top g_{x,noisy}-J^\top g_{x,clean}\|^2
=\underbrace{\|(\widehat J-J)^\top g_{x,clean}\|^2}_{\text{approximation bias}}
+\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}(\widehat J^\top A^*C_\epsilon A\widehat J)}_{\text{noise contribution}}.
\]

For isotropic measurement noise, the second term is sigma squared times ||A hat J||_F squared. The corresponding exact-gradient term is sigma squared times ||AJ||_F squared. DLR can reduce the noise term while increasing bias. This expectation applies to independent noise draws at fixed sources/factors, not automatically to sources optimized from that same random noise. For MRI the equations use the real-linear observation map into real and imaginary acquired coefficients.

## Why continued exact optimization can worsen the image

Locally linearize the data map as AG(z+delta z) approximately AG(z)+AJ delta z. If AJ has singular value lambda_i, plain gradient descent admits that mode of the data with filter

\[
f_i(k)=1-(1-\eta\lambda_i^2)^k.
\]

For positive lambda_i, the corresponding source-noise coefficient is f_i(k)/lambda_i times the noise component in the left singular direction. With 0<eta<=1/||AJ||_2 squared and fixed linearization, these filters grow monotonically: longer optimization progressively fits weakly observed modes. Inverse sensitivity can make these modes costly in image space. LBFGS can accelerate their fitting. Exact gradients of noisy least squares need not minimize image error.

This is the standard iterative-regularization/semiconvergence mechanism; see [Clason, Regularization of Inverse Problems, chapters 5 and 7](https://arxiv.org/html/2001.00617). The present data are consistent with DLR limiting those updates. Establishing the precise retained modes requires measurement-aware spectral diagnostics, not only a rank estimate of J.

## Small same-source CPU diagnostic

We recomputed fixed-source gradients for two illustrative 64-squared cases: CT5% sample15 and MRI20% sample14, at the initial, D-Flow-final, and DLR-final sources. Each evaluation uses the same canonical endpoint and fixed factors for the observed noise plus ten additional independent noise realizations. Fresh sketches and a new evolution seeded by saved terminal V are reported separately. Saved V is the output of the last factor evaluation, not its original input; the warm recomputation is not an exact replay of the optimizer's last gradient.

Define R=||g_DLR,noisy-g_exact,clean||/||g_exact,noisy-g_exact,clean||. At the failed CT D-Flow endpoint (104.06% image error), R=0.555; DLR transmits 0.215 times the exact noise-gradient norm, and the corresponding plain-GD image perturbation has norm ratio 0.052. Nine of ten additional noise draws give R<1 (median 0.392). A native-AD factor control gives R=0.579 with essentially the same attenuation.

However, R is about 300 at that case's initial source, and 1.09 at its successful DLR endpoint (5.05% image error). For the successful MRI DLR endpoint (2.94% error), R is about 1.001. Thus **a uniformly cleaner gradient is not supported**, even in selected cases with good DLR final accuracy.

Eight full-map JVPs also check JV against US. The relative image-operator discrepancy ||J hat J^T-hat J hat J^T||_F/||hat J hat J^T||_F is 0.0091 for the warm CT DLR-final factors and 0.0038 for MRI, but 0.539 and 0.200 at initialization. A positive spectral-filter interpretation can be locally plausible near these endpoints without being valid throughout the trajectory. Small Frobenius discrepancy does not certify positive semidefiniteness or stability of the actual LBFGS operator J H_k hat J^T.

Native JVP/VJP adjoint and projection-identity checks passed. One deliberately poor CT endpoint has 0.17% internal primal drift between fast hand-product propagation and canonical Euler propagation on CPU; the native-AD control preserves the main attenuation finding. CPU checks do not reproduce the original CUDA kernels, and these two cases are illustrative rather than representative. No certified full-Jacobian spectrum was computed in this investigation.

## A rank-only explanation fails in one directly checked case

A bounded randomized full-map spectrum (width32, up to three refinement stages, 16 independent tail probes) was computed at three sources for the CT5% sample15 case. These are spectra of the same true five-step generator derivative at different sources, not spectra of the DLR surrogate.

| Source | Estimated largest singular value of J | Estimated rank for 99.9% squared-singular-value energy |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Initial | 9.95 | 3 |
| D-Flow final, image error104.06% | 20,679 | 1 |
| DLR final, image error5.05% | 176.05 | 3 |

The failed D-Flow endpoint has lower relative effective rank and much greater maximum sensitivity. A single very large singular value makes a relative energy rank look smaller. Thus low effective rank is not equivalent to low noise amplification, and retaining the largest singular values is not automatically a remedy for excessive gain. This case suggests studying both absolute sensitivity and which parts of the source space the trajectories visit. It does not establish the same mechanism across the dataset or a causal effect from endpoint comparisons.

Leading-triplet residuals at the two final sources are below 1e-6 times the estimated largest singular value. The initial99.99% energy cutoff remains unresolved at the width32 budget, and the tail estimates are stochastic. These are effective-energy estimates, not algebraic ranks or full condition numbers. Raw values, convergence diagnostics, and hashes are in `fixed_points/spectra/`.

The extreme gain also passes a directional finite-difference check: FP32 central differences agree with the top-direction derivative to0.47% at step1e-5. An isolated FP64 model copy gives gain20,676.6 and central-difference relative vector error8.5e-8 at step1e-7. The FP64 control locally casts the model's explicitly FP32 time-embedding output to double; production code is unchanged. The large gain is therefore not merely an FP32 autodifferentiation artifact.

## Most informative next experiments

1. **Noise-aware stopping baseline:** compare all three methods with prespecified discrepancy thresholds, retaining all cases and testing threshold sensitivity. This is necessary before claiming a final-accuracy advantage beyond resistance to overoptimization.
2. **Separate subspace from coefficients:** at identical saved sources, compare exact gradient, exact projected gradient VV^T g_exact, true rank-r truncated-J gradient, and actual DLR. Repeat clean/noisy measurements with fixed factors. A GD control separates these effects from LBFGS history.
3. **Measure the observed directions:** estimate spectra of J and AJ, inspect AU and AJV, and map retained/discarded singular directions into images. The image-noise amplification ||JV(AJV)^dagger||_F is relevant to a locally restricted least-squares solve. Rank by itself cannot measure this sensitivity; a rank-deficient surrogate has infinite full condition number, so report the retained nonzero spectrum explicitly.
4. **Distinguish good and bad DLR basins:** include persistent DLR failures and multiple paired sources. Measure whether clean correction energy lies outside span(V). This tests whether regularization also prevents necessary geometric changes.

These comparisons concern our unconstrained common-LBFGS P-Flow variant; the [P-Flow paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.08328v1) also uses source projection. Five Euler steps do not by themselves establish long-chain differentiation as the failure mechanism. The current evidence is stronger for noise fitting than for numerical derivative failure or a proved invariant stable manifold.
