2. Noise Test: RK4 versus Forward Euler

Goal. With the number of time steps matched between integrators, we hope to see that RK4 yields better reconstructions than forward Euler. RK4 uses four velocity evaluations per step against Euler's one, so at matched step count it costs roughly four times as much; the question is whether the reduced time-discretization error buys a corresponding gain in reconstruction quality.

3. The DLR-D-Flow Algorithm

3.1 Setting and Notation

The generative flow

A pretrained flow-matching model defines an ordinary differential equation that transports a noise sample into an image:

\[\dot x_t = v_\theta(t,x_t),\qquad x_0=z,\qquad t\in[0,1].\]

The inverse problem

We observe noisy, incomplete measurements of an unknown true image and want to recover it.

  • \(x_\star\) is the unknown ground-truth image.
  • \(\mathcal{A}\) is the forward operator.
  • \(\eta\) is measurement noise and \(\delta=\|\eta\|_2/\|\mathcal{A}(x_\star)\|_2\) is its relative size.
  • \(y_\delta = \mathcal{A}(x_\star)+\eta\) is the observed data. The subscript records that it carries noise at level \(\delta\); it is not an index.

D-Flow does not optimize the image directly. It optimizes the source point, so that every candidate is by construction an image the flow can generate — this is what enforces the prior:

\[\min_{z}\ \Phi(z),\qquad \Phi(z)=\tfrac12\big\|\mathcal{A}(x_1(z))-y_\delta\big\|_2^2 .\]

\(\Phi\) is the objective: half the squared mismatch between the measurements predicted from the current sample and those actually observed.

The gradient and why it is hard

Minimizing \(\Phi\) needs \(\nabla_z\Phi\), its gradient with respect to the source point. The chain rule splits this into two pieces:

\[\nabla_z\Phi \;=\; J_1^{\top}g_x,\qquad g_x \;=\; D\mathcal{A}(x_1)^{*}\big(\mathcal{A}(x_1)-y_\delta\big).\]
  • \(g_x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the terminal gradient: the derivative of \(\Phi\) with respect to the generated image \(x_1\), holding the flow fixed. Here \(D\mathcal{A}(x_1)\) is the derivative of the forward operator and \(D\mathcal{A}(x_1)^{*}\) its adjoint, which pushes a measurement-space residual back into image space. \(g_x\) is cheap — it involves no flow.
  • \(J_t=\partial x_t/\partial z\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is the flow sensitivity: it records how a perturbation of the source point changes the state at flow time \(t\). \(J_1\) is its value at the endpoint, so \(J_1\) says how the generated image responds to a change in \(z\).
  • \(J_1^{\top}\) is its transpose. Applying it to \(g_x\) pulls the image-space gradient back through the whole flow to become a gradient in source space. This is the expensive object, and the entire method exists to approximate its action.

Differentiating the flow ODE with respect to \(z\) shows that \(J_t\) satisfies its own matrix-valued ODE, the variational or sensitivity equation:

\[\dot J_t = A_tJ_t,\qquad J_0=I,\qquad A_t=\partial_x v_\theta(t,x_t).\]

\(A_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is the Jacobian of the velocity field with respect to its state argument, evaluated along the current trajectory. The initial condition \(J_0=I\) is the identity because at \(t=0\) the state is the source point, so perturbing \(z\) perturbs \(x_0\) one-for-one.

The obstacle. At 160×160, \(d=25{,}600\), so \(J_t\) and \(A_t\) are each \(25{,}600\times25{,}600\) — and \(A_t\) is the Jacobian of a neural network, never available as an array. Neither matrix can be formed or stored. What is available cheaply is the action of \(A_t\) on a few vectors: \(A_tQ\) by a forward-mode derivative (JVP) and \(A_t^{\top}Q\) by a reverse-mode one (VJP), for a skinny \(Q\) with \(r\ll d\) columns. Every step of the algorithm is built from those two products.

3.2 Deriving the \(U_t\), \(S_t\), \(V_t\) Equations

Model the sensitivity as a scalar baseline plus a rank-\(r\) residual,

\[J_t \approx \alpha_t I + Y_t,\qquad Y_t = U_tS_tV_t^{\top},\qquad U_t,V_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ S_t\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\]

with orthonormal factors \(U_t^{\top}U_t=V_t^{\top}V_t=I_r\). Here \(\alpha_t\in\mathbb{R}\) is a single scalar — the baseline — and \(Y_t\) is the rank-\(r\) residual that corrects it.

What \(\alpha_t\) is and why it is there

A pure rank-\(r\) model cannot represent the initial condition. The sensitivity starts at \(J_0=I\), which has full rank \(d\), so no \(USV^{\top}\) with \(r\ll d\) can approximate it. Splitting off a multiple of the identity fixes this: at \(t=0\) we take \(\alpha_0=1\) and \(Y_0=0\), so the model reproduces \(J_0=I\) exactly, and the low-rank part only has to describe how the sensitivity deviates from a scalar multiple of the identity as the flow proceeds.

The natural choice for that scalar is the one that best matches \(J_t\) in the Frobenius norm. Minimizing \(\|J_t-\alpha I\|_F\) over \(\alpha\) gives the mean diagonal entry,

\[\alpha_t \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{tr}(J_t)}{d},\]

so \(\alpha_t\) measures the average factor by which the flow stretches or contracts a perturbation of the source point, ignoring any directional structure.

The trace of \(J_t\) cannot be read off directly, so it is estimated stochastically. We draw \(k\) fixed random probe vectors \(p_1,\dots,p_k\) with entries \(\pm1\), carry them along the flow by the same sensitivity equation, \(q_i(t)=J_tp_i\), and use the Hutchinson estimator

\[\alpha_t \;\approx\; \frac{1}{k\,d}\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i^{\top}q_i(t),\qquad \dot\alpha_t \;\approx\; \frac{1}{k\,d}\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i^{\top}A_tq_i(t),\]

which is unbiased because \(\mathbb{E}\,p^{\top}Mp=\operatorname{tr}(M)\) for such probes. The implementation uses \(k=32\). Propagating the probes costs one extra product \(A_tQ\) per flow step; \(\dot\alpha_t\) comes free from the same product, and both feed the factor updates below.

In practice the baseline contributes very little. The trained flows here are strongly contractive, so \(\alpha_t\) decays from 1 to roughly 0.008 by \(t=1\): almost all of the sensitivity is carried by the low-rank residual, not by the scalar. Setting the terminal baseline to zero changes the relative gradient error only from 0.1287 to 0.1288. The baseline is therefore doing real work near \(t=0\), where \(J_t\) is still close to the identity, and essentially none by the time the gradient is read off. Note also that integrating the log-volume relation \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\alpha_t=\operatorname{tr}(A_t)/d\) would give \(\det(J_t)^{1/d}\), the geometric mean of the singular values, which is not the Frobenius-optimal scalar and is substantially smaller; the implementation uses \(\operatorname{tr}(J_t)/d\) above.

Substituting the model into the sensitivity equation gives the residual dynamics

\[\dot Y_t = F(t,Y_t) := A_t\big(\alpha_tI+Y_t\big)-\dot\alpha_t I.\]

The right-hand side \(F\) generally leaves the rank-\(r\) manifold \(\mathcal{M}_r\). The Koch–Lubich construction imposes a Galerkin condition: choose the velocity within the tangent space that is closest to the true one,

\[\dot Y_t \in T_{Y_t}\mathcal{M}_r \quad\text{such that}\quad \big\|\dot Y_t - F(t,Y_t)\big\|_F \ \text{is minimal}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \dot Y_t = P_{T_{Y_t}\mathcal{M}_r}F(t,Y_t).\]

Every tangent vector at \(Y=USV^{\top}\) has the form

\[\delta Y = \delta U\,S\,V^{\top} + U\,\delta S\,V^{\top} + U\,S\,\delta V^{\top},\]

and this representation is not unique — the decomposition admits rotations that leave \(Y\) unchanged. Uniqueness is fixed by the gauge conditions

\[U^{\top}\dot U = 0,\qquad V^{\top}\dot V = 0,\]

which also preserve orthonormality, since \(\frac{d}{dt}(U^{\top}U)=\dot U^{\top}U+U^{\top}\dot U=0\). With these conditions the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space is

\[P_Y(Z) = ZVV^{\top} - UU^{\top}ZVV^{\top} + UU^{\top}Z.\]

Now write \(\dot Y = \dot U S V^{\top} + U\dot S V^{\top} + U S\dot V^{\top}\) and extract each factor equation by projecting.

Core. Multiply on the left by \(U^{\top}\) and on the right by \(V\). The gauge conditions kill the outer terms, \(U^{\top}\dot U=0\) and \(\dot V^{\top}V=0\), leaving

\[U^{\top}\dot Y V = \dot S \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\ \dot S = U^{\top} Z V\ }\qquad (Z:=F(t,Y)).\]

Left factor. Multiply on the right by \(V\) only: \(\dot Y V = \dot U S + U\dot S\). Applying \(I-UU^{\top}\) removes the second term, and \((I-UU^{\top})\dot U = \dot U\) by the gauge condition, so

\[\dot U S = (I-UU^{\top})ZV \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\ \dot U = (I-UU^{\top})\,Z\,V\,S^{-1}\ }.\]

Right factor. Symmetrically, multiplying on the left by \(U^{\top}\) and applying \(I-VV^{\top}\) to the transpose gives

\[\boxed{\ \dot V = (I-VV^{\top})\,Z^{\top}\,U\,S^{-\top}\ }.\]
Why these equations are not integrated directly. Both factor equations contain \(S^{-1}\). The core \(S_t\) becomes ill-conditioned exactly when the approximation is nearly of lower rank — which is the normal situation here, and is the situation at \(t=0\) where the residual starts at \(Y_0=0\). A direct integrator would need a step size tied to the smallest singular value of \(S\).

3.3 Projector Splitting

The Lubich–Oseledets integrator avoids \(S^{-1}\) entirely by splitting the projection into its three terms and integrating each in turn. Over one flow step of length \(h\) with \(A_t\) and \(\alpha_t\) frozen:

\[P_Y(Z) = \underbrace{ZVV^{\top}}_{\text{K-step}} \underbrace{-\,UU^{\top}ZVV^{\top}}_{\text{S-step}} + \underbrace{UU^{\top}Z}_{\text{L-step}}\]
  • K-step. Freeze \(V=V_0\), set \(K=US\) and integrate \(\dot K = F(t,KV_0^{\top})V_0\). A thin QR factorization \(K(t_1)=U_1\hat S\) produces the new column space.
  • S-step. Freeze both bases and integrate \(\dot S = -U_1^{\top}F(t,U_1SV_0^{\top})V_0\). The minus sign makes this substep run backwards; it removes the double counting between the other two.
  • L-step. Freeze \(U=U_1\), set \(L=VS^{\top}\) and integrate \(\dot L = F(t,U_1L^{\top})^{\top}U_1\), then \(L(t_1)=V_1S_1^{\top}\) by QR.

For the scalar-baseline model \(F(t,Y)=A_t(\alpha_tI+Y)-\dot\alpha_tI\) these become

\[\dot K = \alpha_tA_tV_0 + A_tK - \dot\alpha_tV_0,\qquad \dot S = -\alpha_tU_1^{\top}A_tV_0 - (U_1^{\top}A_tU_1)S + \dot\alpha_tU_1^{\top}V_0,\]
\[\dot L = \alpha_tA_t^{\top}U_1 + L\,(U_1^{\top}A_t^{\top}U_1) - \dot\alpha_tU_1.\]

Every occurrence of \(A_t\) is applied to a matrix with only \(r\) columns. Those products are computed as batched JVPs and VJPs through the velocity network, so the Jacobian is never assembled. Per flow step the cost is \(O(r)\) network evaluations, \(O(dr^2)\) for the thin QRs, and \(O(r^3)\) for the core algebra; storage is \(O(dr)\) rather than \(O(d^2)\).

Initialization. The residual begins at \(Y_0=0\), which has no singular vectors, so the factorization is undefined at \(t=0\). Seeding \(S_0=\varepsilon I\) on a random basis fails badly: with \(S\approx0\) the tangent projection is degenerate and the basis never rotates toward the directions that carry the gradient. The implementation instead defers initialization by one Euler step and forms a randomized sketch of the first genuinely nonzero residual \(Y_h\approx hA_0+(1-\alpha_h)I\), then augments the bases with fresh forcing directions at each step before recompressing to rank \(r\). This single change reduced relative gradient error at 64×64 from 0.85 to 0.13.

3.4 Algorithm

Algorithm 1 — DLR-D-Flow
Input: observation \(y\); operator \(\mathcal{A}\); velocity field \(v_\theta\); initial source \(z_0\); rank \(r\); flow steps \(N\) with \(h=1/N\); outer steps \(K\); step size \(\eta\)
Output: the iterate with the smallest measurement misfit
  1. outer for \(k=1,\dots,K\):
    1. init Set \(x\leftarrow z\), \(\alpha\leftarrow1\). Take one Euler step and sketch the first nonzero residual, since \(Y_0=0\) has no singular vectors:
      \[(U,S,V)\leftarrow\operatorname{rand-svd}_r\big(Z\mapsto hA_0Z+(1-\alpha_h)Z\big),\qquad x\leftarrow x+h\,v_\theta(0,x).\]
    2. flow for \(n=1,\dots,N-1\) with \(t=nh\), using only \(A_tQ\) (JVP) and \(A_t^{\top}Q\) (VJP):
      1. K-step. \(K_1=US+h\big(\alpha A_tV+A_t US-\dot\alpha V\big)\), then \(U_1\hat S=\operatorname{qr}(K_1)\). new column space
      2. S-step. With \(M=U_1^{\top}A_tU_1\), \(\tilde S=\hat S+h\big(-\alpha U_1^{\top}A_tV-M\hat S+\dot\alpha U_1^{\top}V\big)\). runs backwards; removes double counting
      3. L-step. \(L_1=V\tilde S^{\top}+h\big(\alpha A_t^{\top}U_1+V\tilde S^{\top}M^{\top}-\dot\alpha U_1\big)\), then \(V_1S_1^{\top}=\operatorname{qr}(L_1)\). new row space
      4. Enrich and recompress. Augment the bases with fresh forcing directions and truncate back to rank \(r\):\(\ (U,S,V)\leftarrow\operatorname{trunc}_r\big([U_1\,|\,A_tU_1],\,S_1,\,[V_1\,|\,A_t^{\top}V_1]\big)\). keeps the basis from going stale
      5. Advance. \(x\leftarrow x+h\,v_\theta(t,x)\), and update \(\alpha\) from the propagated probes.
    3. grad Terminal gradient and its low-rank pullback:
      \[g_x=D\mathcal{A}(x)^{*}\big(\mathcal{A}(x)-y\big),\qquad \widehat{\nabla_z\Phi}=\alpha g_x+VS^{\top}U^{\top}g_x.\]
      three small mat-vecs; \(J_1\) is never formed
    4. step \(z\leftarrow\operatorname{adam}\big(z,\widehat{\nabla_z\Phi},\eta\big)\), then regenerate \(x_k=\operatorname{flow}(z)\) and retain it if \(\|\mathcal{A}(x_k)-y\|^2\) is the smallest seen.

Per flow step: \(O(r)\) network evaluations, two thin QR factorizations of \(d\times r\) matrices, and \(r\times r\) core algebra. Storage is \(O(dr)\).
