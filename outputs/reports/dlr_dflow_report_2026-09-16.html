1. Noise Test at 200×200 and 250×250: RK4 versus Forward Euler

Goal. Unchanged from the 9 September report: at 128 and 160 with matched step counts there was no clear advantage for RK4, so the study was extended to the two largest grids, 200×200 and 250×250, at 1% measurement noise. RK4 uses four velocity evaluations per step against Euler's one, so at matched step count it costs about four times as much; the question is whether the reduced time-discretization error buys a corresponding gain in reconstruction quality. 2. From D-Flow to DLR-D-Flow

The two methods solve the same optimization problem with the same outer loop. They differ in exactly one step. This section states D-Flow first, then that one step, then the machinery behind it, and finally the numerical comparison.

2.1 D-Flow

A pretrained flow-matching model transports a source point into an image along

\[\dot x_t=v_\theta(t,x_t),\qquad x_0=z,\qquad t\in[0,1],\]

discretized here as \(N=100\) forward-Euler steps of size \(h=1/N\). Given noisy, incomplete data \(y_\delta=\mathcal{A}(x_\star)+\eta\), D-Flow does not optimize the image. It optimizes the source point, so that every candidate is by construction something the flow can generate — that is what enforces the prior:

\[\min_z\ \Phi(z),\qquad \Phi(z)=\tfrac12\big\|\mathcal{A}(x_1(z))-y_\delta\big\|_2^2 .\]

The chain rule splits the gradient of \(\Phi\) into a cheap piece and an expensive one:

\[\nabla_z\Phi=\underbrace{J_1^{\top}g_x}_{\text{expensive}},\qquad g_x=\underbrace{D\mathcal{A}(x_1)^{*}\big(\mathcal{A}(x_1)-y_\delta\big)}_{\text{cheap: no flow involved}} .\]
  • \(g_x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the terminal gradient: the derivative of \(\Phi\) with respect to the generated image, holding the flow fixed.
  • \(J_t=\partial x_t/\partial z\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is the flow sensitivity: how a perturbation of the source point changes the state at time \(t\). Applying \(J_1^{\top}\) to \(g_x\) pulls the image-space gradient back through the entire flow into source space.

D-Flow performs that pullback exactly, by reverse-mode differentiation through the ODE solver: the forward pass records every intermediate activation of all \(N\) steps, and the backward pass sweeps through them. This is the classical adjoint computation, and it never forms \(J_1\).

Algorithm 1 — D-Flow (exact gradient)
Input: data \(y_\delta\); operator \(\mathcal{A}\); velocity field \(v_\theta\); initial source \(z_0\); flow steps \(N\); outer steps \(K\)
Output: the iterate with the smallest measurement misfit
  1. outer for \(k=1,\dots,K\):
    1. Generate. Run the flow \(x_1=\operatorname{flow}(z)\), recording the activation tape for all \(N\) steps. \(N\) network evaluations
    2. Terminal gradient. \(g_x=D\mathcal{A}(x_1)^{*}\big(\mathcal{A}(x_1)-y_\delta\big)\).
    3. Exact pullback. \(\nabla_z\Phi=J_1^{\top}g_x\) by reverse-mode differentiation over the tape. about \(2N\) further network evaluations
    4. Step. \(z\leftarrow\operatorname{LBFGS}(z,\nabla_z\Phi)\); keep \(x_1\) if its misfit is the smallest seen.
What this costs. About \(3N\approx300\) network evaluations per outer iteration, and an activation tape for the whole flow — 25.7 GB at 128×128, and beyond the card above that, which is why the runs at 160 and larger use activation checkpointing (recompute instead of store, at roughly double the compute).

2.2 DLR-D-Flow: approximating the sensitivity

DLR-D-Flow keeps Algorithm 1 intact and replaces step (c). Instead of differentiating back through the solver, it carries a cheap surrogate for the sensitivity forward alongside the state, so that at \(t=1\) an approximate pullback is available immediately. The surrogate is a scalar multiple of the identity plus a rank-\(r\) correction,

\[J_t\ \approx\ \alpha_tI+Y_t,\qquad Y_t=U_tS_tV_t^{\top},\qquad U_t,V_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ S_t\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\ r\ll d,\]

with \(\alpha_t\in\mathbb{R}\) a single tracked scalar. Transposing and applying it to \(g_x\) gives the approximate gradient that replaces the exact one:

\[\widehat{\nabla_z\Phi}\ =\ \alpha_1g_x\;+\;V_1S_1^{\top}U_1^{\top}g_x .\]

Read right to left, the second term costs three skinny matrix–vector products: \(U_1^{\top}g_x\) compresses the image-space gradient to \(r\) numbers, \(S_1^{\top}\) mixes them, and \(V_1\) expands back to source space. The first term passes \(g_x\) straight through, scaled. Nothing of size \(d\times d\) is ever formed, and no tape is kept.

Algorithm 2 — DLR-D-Flow (approximate gradient)
Input: as Algorithm 1, plus rank \(r\)
Output: the iterate with the smallest measurement misfit
  1. outer for \(k=1,\dots,K\):
    1. Generate and evolve together. Run the flow \(x_1=\operatorname{flow}(z)\) with no tape, and on the same time grid evolve the factors \((U_t,S_t,V_t)\) and the scalar \(\alpha_t\) from \(\alpha_0=1,\ Y_0=0\). detailed in §2.3–2.4
    2. Terminal gradient. \(g_x=D\mathcal{A}(x_1)^{*}\big(\mathcal{A}(x_1)-y_\delta\big)\). identical to Algorithm 1
    3. Approximate pullback. \(\widehat{\nabla_z\Phi}=\alpha_1g_x+V_1S_1^{\top}U_1^{\top}g_x\). three skinny mat-vecs; this is the only step that differs
    4. Safeguarded step. \(z\leftarrow\operatorname{LBFGS}(z,\widehat{\nabla_z\Phi})\); if \(\Phi\) is not finite or exceeds four times the best value so far, roll back to the best iterate, halve the step size and discard the curvature history.

2.3 How the approximation is built

Why a plain rank-\(r\) model is not enough

The obvious idea is to compress the sensitivity directly, \(J_t\approx U_tS_tV_t^{\top}\). This fails at the very first step. The flow map of a smooth ODE is locally invertible, so \(J_t\) is generally of full rank, and at \(t=0\)

\[J_0=I,\]

because the state is the source point there, so perturbing \(z\) perturbs \(x_0\) one-for-one. No rank-\(r\) matrix approximates the identity when \(r\ll d\).

The assumption that is justified is weaker: not that \(J_t\) is rank-deficient, but that its action is effectively low-rank — the part that matters here, \(J_t^{\top}g_x\), is concentrated in a few directions. That is the same intuition that makes D-Flow work at all, namely that differentiating through the flow projects the terminal gradient onto dominant data-manifold directions. Hence a low-rank plus baseline model: the scalar carries the full-rank part the factorization structurally cannot, and the rank-\(r\) residual carries the directional structure.

The scalar

Starting from \(\alpha_0=1,\ Y_0=0\) reproduces \(J_0=I\) exactly, so the residual only has to describe how the sensitivity deviates from a multiple of the identity. Holding \(\alpha_t\equiv1\) gives a poor sensitivity action on these flows, which are strongly contractive; instead \(\alpha_t\) is the scalar that best matches \(J_t\) in Frobenius norm, which is the mean diagonal entry

\[\alpha_t=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(J_t)}{d}.\]

The trace is not directly available, so it is estimated stochastically: draw \(k_p\) fixed \(\pm1\) probes \(p_i\), carry them along the flow by the same sensitivity equation so that \(q_i(t)=J_tp_i\), and take

\[\alpha_t\approx\frac{1}{k_pd}\sum_{i=1}^{k_p}p_i^{\top}q_i(t),\qquad\dot\alpha_t\approx\frac{1}{k_pd}\sum_{i=1}^{k_p}p_i^{\top}A_tq_i(t),\]

unbiased because \(\mathbb{E}\,p^{\top}Mp=\operatorname{tr}(M)\) for such probes. Propagating the probes costs one extra Jacobian product per flow step, and \(\dot\alpha_t\) comes free from that same product.

What the residual has to satisfy

Differentiating the flow ODE with respect to \(z\) shows that the sensitivity obeys its own matrix-valued ODE, the variational equation,

\[\dot J_t=A_tJ_t,\qquad J_0=I,\qquad A_t=\partial_xv_\theta(t,x_t),\]

where \(A_t\) is the Jacobian of the velocity field along the current trajectory. At 250×250, \(d=62{,}500\), so \(A_t\) and \(J_t\) are each \(62{,}500\times62{,}500\) and neither can be formed — \(A_t\) is a neural network Jacobian and is never available as an array. What is cheap is its action on a few vectors: \(A_tQ\) by a forward-mode derivative (JVP) and \(A_t^{\top}Q\) by a reverse-mode one (VJP), for skinny \(Q\). Everything below uses only those two products.

Substituting the surrogate into the variational equation gives the dynamics the residual must follow:

\[\dot Y_t=F(t,Y_t):=A_t\big(\alpha_tI+Y_t\big)-\dot\alpha_tI.\]

Keeping the residual on the rank-\(r\) manifold

\(F\) generally points off the rank-\(r\) manifold \(\mathcal{M}_r\), so the Koch–Lubich construction takes the velocity within the tangent space closest to the true one,

\[\dot Y_t=P_{T_{Y_t}\mathcal{M}_r}F(t,Y_t),\qquad P_Y(Z)=ZVV^{\top}-UU^{\top}ZVV^{\top}+UU^{\top}Z.\]

Expanding this factor by factor, with the gauge conditions \(U^{\top}\dot U=0\) and \(V^{\top}\dot V=0\) that make the representation unique and preserve orthonormality, gives

\[\dot S=U^{\top}ZV,\qquad\dot U=(I-UU^{\top})ZVS^{-1},\qquad\dot V=(I-VV^{\top})Z^{\top}US^{-\top},\qquad Z:=F(t,Y).\]
Why these are not integrated directly. Both factor equations contain \(S^{-1}\), and the core is ill-conditioned exactly when the approximation is nearly of lower rank — the normal situation here, and precisely the situation at \(t=0\) where \(Y_0=0\). A direct integrator would need a step size tied to the smallest singular value of \(S\).

The Lubich–Oseledets projector-splitting integrator removes \(S^{-1}\) entirely by splitting the projection into its three terms and integrating each in turn over one flow step, with \(A_t\) and \(\alpha_t\) frozen:

\[P_Y(Z)=\underbrace{ZVV^{\top}}_{\text{K-step}}\underbrace{-\,UU^{\top}ZVV^{\top}}_{\text{S-step}}+\underbrace{UU^{\top}Z}_{\text{L-step}}\]

The K-step finds the new column space, the L-step the new row space, and the S-step runs backwards between them to remove the double counting. For the scalar-plus-low-rank model the three substeps are

\[\dot K=\alpha_tA_tV+A_tK-\dot\alpha_tV,\qquad \dot S=-\alpha_tU_1^{\top}A_tV-(U_1^{\top}A_tU_1)S+\dot\alpha_tU_1^{\top}V,\]
\[\dot L=\alpha_tA_t^{\top}U_1+L\,(U_1^{\top}A_t^{\top}U_1)-\dot\alpha_tU_1,\]

each started from the current factors and closed by a thin QR factorization. Every occurrence of \(A_t\) acts on a matrix with only \(r\) columns.

Starting the recursion

One difficulty remains at \(t=0\): the residual starts at \(Y_0=0\), which has no singular vectors, so the factorization is undefined there. Seeding \(S_0=\varepsilon I\) on a random basis fails badly — with \(S\approx0\) the tangent projection is degenerate and the basis never rotates toward the directions that carry the gradient. The implementation instead defers initialization by one Euler step and forms a randomized sketch of the first genuinely nonzero residual,

\[Y_h\approx hA_0+(1-\alpha_h)I,\]

then at each subsequent step augments the bases with fresh forcing directions before recompressing back to rank \(r\), which keeps the basis from going stale as the trajectory turns. This single change reduced the relative gradient error at 64×64 from 0.85 to 0.13.

2.4 One flow step of the factor evolution

This is step (a) of Algorithm 2, expanded. It is what runs 100 times per outer iteration.

Algorithm 3 — factor evolution, one step from \(t\) to \(t+h\)
Have: state \(x\), factors \((U,S,V)\), scalar \(\alpha\), probes \(Q\)
Use only: \(A_tQ\) (JVP) and \(A_t^{\top}Q\) (VJP)
  1. K-step. \(K_1=US+h\big(\alpha A_tV+A_tUS-\dot\alpha V\big)\), then \(U_1\hat S=\operatorname{qr}(K_1)\). new column space
  2. S-step. with \(M=U_1^{\top}A_tU_1\), \(\tilde S=\hat S+h\big(-\alpha U_1^{\top}A_tV-M\hat S+\dot\alpha U_1^{\top}V\big)\). backward substep
  3. L-step. \(L_1=V\tilde S^{\top}+h\big(\alpha A_t^{\top}U_1+V\tilde S^{\top}M^{\top}-\dot\alpha U_1\big)\), then \(V_1S_1^{\top}=\operatorname{qr}(L_1)\). new row space
  4. Enrich and recompress. Augment \(U_1,V_1\) with \(p\) fresh forcing directions and truncate the augmented core back to rank \(r\) by a small SVD.
  5. Advance. \(x\leftarrow x+h\,v_\theta(t,x)\); propagate the probes and update \(\alpha,\dot\alpha\) by the Hutchinson estimates above.

Per step: \(O(r+k_p+p)\) Jacobian products through the velocity network, two thin QR factorizations of \(d\times r\) matrices, and \(r\times r\) core algebra. Storage \(O(dr+r^2)\), independent of \(N\).

Settings used in the experiment below: \(r=8\), \(N=100\), \(K=100\), \(k_p=8\) probes, \(p=4\) enrichment directions, \(\lambda=10^{-3}\), LBFGS learning rate 1.0 with history 100. The terminal scalar is set to \(\alpha_1=0\) in the pullback: these flows are strongly contractive, \(\alpha_t\) decays from 1 to order \(10^{-3}\) by \(t=1\), and there \(J_1\) is numerically low rank so the identity term is mostly error. A diagnostic this week qualifies that default — at iterates that have drifted off the data distribution the spectrum of \(J_1\) flattens, and retaining \(\alpha_1\) halves the gradient action error (relative error 0.23 → 0.10, cosine 0.976 → 0.995) at no extra cost, since \(\alpha_t\) is already tracked.

2.5 How close the surrogate gradient is, and what the method claims

The quantity to check is not how well \(\alpha_1I+Y_1\) approximates \(J_1\) as a matrix, but how well its action reproduces the gradient. On fresh factors at rank 8 the relative action error

\[\frac{\big\|J_1^{\top}g_x-\big(\alpha_1g_x+V_1S_1^{\top}U_1^{\top}g_x\big)\big\|_2}{\|J_1^{\top}g_x\|_2}\]

is 0.066 with cosine 0.998 against the exact gradient, falling to 0.030 and 0.9997 at rank 16. The direction is therefore accurate where the factors are fresh, which is why they are rebuilt from \(t=0\) at every outer iteration: measurements show the alignment decays within about two iterations if they are reused.

The comparison to keep in mind. For a single scalar objective and a single gradient evaluation, the classical adjoint equation \(\dot a_t=-A_t^{\top}a_t,\ a_1=g_x\), already returns the exact gradient without ever forming \(J_t\) — and that is precisely what reverse-mode differentiation implements in Algorithm 1. DLR-D-Flow should therefore not be advertised as automatically cheaper than one exact adjoint gradient when \(r>1\). Its computational role is more specific: it removes the full-Jacobian and repeated-SVD bottleneck, and it produces a reusable low-rank sensitivity operator, so several gradient or trial-step applications can be made cheaply once the factors exist. The experiment below measures it against the single-vector adjoint, which is the hardest baseline for this method and the one that matters in practice.
What it does and does not guarantee. The method removes the full-SVD bottleneck; it does not make the exact flow Jacobian low rank. It is useful when the update only needs the dominant sensitivity directions, and if the forward operator needs information in directions the rank-\(r\) model discards the update becomes biased. The factors are therefore best treated as an approximate gradient mechanism or a structured preconditioner, and an objective-decrease check must stay in the solver — which is the safeguard in step (d) of Algorithm 2.

2.6 Numerical Experiment

Protocol. Five independent samples per resolution (matching Part 1's design), each method started from the same source point per sample. Rank 8, 32 trace probes, 4 enrichment probes, factors re-evolved from \(t=0\) every outer iteration. LBFGS (lr 1.0, max_iter 1, history 100, no line search) minimises the data misfit alone — no source prior this week, unlike Part 1 and the 9 September benchmark, which both used \(\lambda\|z\|_2^2\) with \(\lambda=10^{-3}\). Each run stops at a 1% relative measurement residual (the stop loss) or a 200-iteration budget, whichever comes first. D-Flow uses activation checkpointing at 160×160 and above; DLR-D-Flow uses CUDA-graph replay of the product calls. 250×250 is excluded: its DLR-D-Flow arm has not finished (each attempt approaches ten hours at this configuration).

(ckpt) — at 160×160 and 200×200 D-Flow recomputes each step's forward pass during the backward pass instead of storing the activation tape, which is why its peak memory (0.7–1.3 GB) is now lower than DLR-D-Flow's (3.1–4.8 GB) at those two resolutions — the reverse of the pattern at 64–128, where D-Flow holds the full tape (4.9–25.8 GB) and DLR-D-Flow does not (0.4–1.6 GB).

Outcome. The per-iteration cost ratio runs 1.8–22× and grows with resolution. DLR-D-Flow has the lower median image error (of the three samples DLR ranks best/median/worst) in 2 of the 5 resolutions. The more striking pattern is in the worst case, checked across all five samples at each resolution (not just the three in Table 5): with no source prior this week, D-Flow's LBFGS iterate occasionally locks onto a source point that fits the sparse 15-view sinogram to under 1% residual while reconstructing a non-physical image — 4 such reconstructions (image error 164–195%) occur across 3 of the 5 resolutions (128, 160 and 200), against 0 for DLR-D-Flow at any resolution. Two of these are visible in Table 5 and the gallery below, because they happen to be DLR's median sample at 128×128 and DLR's worst sample at 200×200. DLR-D-Flow is not immune to large errors either (its own worst sample reaches 63% at 160×160), but none of its errors are of this non-physical, sinogram-fitting kind. Neither arm reliably reaches the 1% stop loss above 96×96 within the 200-iteration budget.
Where the cost comes from. The ratio is arithmetic, not implementation slack, and it grows with resolution because the model's attention blocks, at the deepest level, dominate the per-column cost there. D-Flow propagates a single vector backward once through the flow; DLR-D-Flow propagates an operator forward and backward across roughly 60 columns at every one of the 100 flow steps. The memory picture (Table 5) favours DLR-D-Flow at every resolution shown, including 160 and 200 where checkpointed D-Flow was cheaper in the 9 September comparison — that comparison used a fixed 100-iteration budget, while the stop-loss protocol here lets D-Flow's checkpointed runs finish (and re-peak) at very different iteration counts per sample.
